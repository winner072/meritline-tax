Form 5472 With No Income: Do You Still Need to File?

By Mmesoma Winner, Managing DirectorNovember 3, 20268 min read

In most cases, yes. A foreign-owned U.S. LLC with no income usually still has to file Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120. The filing does not depend on profit or revenue. It depends on whether the LLC had transactions with its owner or other related parties, and almost every LLC has at least one. This guide explains the test, shows which zero-income situations still need a return, and covers the rare year when no filing is due.

Key points

  • The test is transactions, not income. Money or payments between you and the LLC can trigger the filing even with $0 in sales.
  • The first year almost always needs a return, because formation costs and first funding are reportable.
  • The penalty is the same with no income: $25,000 per form, per year.
  • A truly dormant year is rare. It means nothing at all moved between you and the LLC, including state fees you paid personally.

Why income does not decide it

Many owners assume that a tax return is only needed when there is income to tax. Form 5472 works differently. It is an information return: it reports dealings between a U.S. company and its foreign owner, so the IRS can see them. It calculates no tax, which is why a company with no revenue can still have something to report.

The IRS instructions say a reporting corporation must file if it had a reportable transaction with a foreign or domestic related party. For a foreign-owned single-member LLC, the instructions specifically include amounts paid or received in connection with forming, dissolving, acquiring, or disposing of the entity, as well as contributions to and distributions from it. None of these are income, and all of them count.

What counts, even with zero revenue

  • Formation costs you paid personally, such as state filing fees or a formation service.
  • Money you put into the LLC, including the first deposit that opened its bank account.
  • Company costs you paid yourself, such as a registered agent, annual report, website, or software bill paid from your own card.
  • Money you took out of the LLC, including repaying yourself for earlier costs.
  • Loans between you and the LLC, in either direction.

Report each one in the part of the form where it belongs. Formation costs, contributions, and distributions go in Part V with a statement, as shown in our completed Form 5472 example. Loans and payments for services go in Part IV.

The first year almost always needs a return

An LLC does not form itself. Someone paid the state fee, and that someone is usually the owner. That is why the formation year is the one where a return is nearly always required, even if the business never made a sale. It is also the year most often missed, because owners assume there is nothing to report until the company starts earning. See how the first year's dates work in Form 5472 tax year and due dates.

Truly dormant years

The instructions say no Form 5472 is required if the LLC had no reportable transactions of the types listed in Parts IV, V, and VI. A later year can genuinely meet that test, but it is less common than owners expect. Before you decide that a year was dormant, check these:

  1. Who paid the state fees?A Delaware annual tax or a Wyoming annual report paid from your own card is a payment for the LLC.
  2. Who paid the registered agent?The same applies to this yearly fee.
  3. Did the bank account change?Review every statement for transfers to or from you.
  4. Did you reimburse yourself?Taking back money you spent earlier is still a transaction.

If a year truly had none of these, keep the records that show why, including the bank statements. If the fees were paid by the LLC from its own balance and nothing else moved, have the year reviewed before you decide, because the facts matter.

State filings do not stop. Even in a dormant year, the state where the LLC was formed may still require its annual report or tax. See our state filings service.

What a no-income return looks like

A return for an LLC with no income is usually short, but it must still be complete:

  • Pro forma Form 1120: name, address, EIN, and item E, with "Foreign-owned U.S. DE" written across the top.
  • Form 5472 Parts I, II, and III: the LLC, you as the foreign owner, and you again as the related party.
  • Part V: the box checked, with a statement listing the formation costs and any contributions or distributions.
  • Part VII: the additional questions, which every filer must answer.

A substantially incomplete form can be treated as not filed, even when the amounts are small. The deadline is the same as for any other year: April 15, or October 15 with Form 7004. See the Form 5472 deadline guide.

If you skipped a year because there was no income

This is one of the most common reasons owners miss Form 5472, and it can be put right. Each missed year that had reportable transactions is a separate late return, with a penalty of $25,000 per form. Filing before the IRS contacts you generally gives you the strongest position.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to file Form 5472 if my LLC had no income?

Usually yes. The filing depends on reportable transactions between the LLC and its owner or other related parties, not on income. Paying formation fees, funding the bank account, or taking money out can all trigger it, even with $0 in revenue.

Do I need to file Form 5472 if my LLC was inactive all year?

Only if there were reportable transactions. If nothing at all moved between you and the LLC, no Form 5472 may be required for that year, but state fees or registered agent fees you paid personally can count, so check the whole year first.

Is the penalty lower if my LLC earned nothing?

No. The penalty is $25,000 per form, per year, for a late, missing, or substantially incomplete Form 5472. It does not depend on income or tax owed.

Will I owe U.S. tax if I file with no income?

Form 5472 calculates no tax. A foreign-owned single-member LLC with no income generally has no U.S. income tax to pay, but the information return may still be required.

My LLC never opened a bank account. Do I still file?

Often yes in the formation year, because the owner usually paid the state filing fee or formation costs personally, and those are reportable. Later years depend on whether any payments were made between you and the LLC.
