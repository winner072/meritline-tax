U.S. LLC Tax Glossary for Foreign Owners: 52 Terms Explained in Plain English

By Mmesoma Winner, Managing DirectorOctober 25, 202611 min read

U.S. tax rules for foreign-owned LLCs come with their own vocabulary, and a single misunderstood word can lead to a missed filing. This glossary explains the 52 terms our clients ask about most, in plain English, grouped by topic. Each definition says what the term means for you as a foreign owner, with a link to the full guide where there is one.

Your company: structure and formation

Articles of Organization

The document filed with a state to create an LLC. Delaware calls it a Certificate of Formation. It shows the LLC's legal name, state, and formation date, which you need for your EIN and every federal filing.

Operating agreement

The internal contract setting out who owns the LLC and how it is run. Most states do not require you to file it, but banks and payment platforms often ask to see it.

Single-member LLC

An LLC with one owner. For U.S. tax purposes it is a disregarded entity by default, unless it elects to be taxed as a corporation. A single-member LLC owned by a foreign person files Form 5472 each year.

Read the full guide →

Multi-member LLC

An LLC with two or more owners. By default it is taxed as a partnership and files Form 1065, not Form 5472.

Read the full guide →

Disregarded entity

A business that the IRS ignores for income tax, treating its income as the owner's own. A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity by default. The LLC still exists legally and can still have its own filing duties.

Read the full guide →

Foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity

A U.S. LLC wholly owned by one foreign person. Since 2017, it is treated as a corporation for Form 5472 reporting and must file Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120. The IRS asks for "Foreign-owned U.S. DE" to be written across the top of the return.

Read the full guide →

Form 8832 (entity classification election)

The form an LLC files to choose how it is taxed, often called the "check-the-box" election. A single-member LLC can use it to be taxed as a corporation instead of a disregarded entity.

Registered agent

The person or company with a physical address in your LLC's state that receives legal and state mail on its behalf. Every LLC must keep one, and the fee is usually paid yearly.

Read the full guide →

Federal filings for your LLC

Form 5472

The IRS information return that reports transactions between a U.S. company and its foreign owner or other foreign related parties. For a foreign-owned single-member LLC it is filed every year, attached to a pro forma Form 1120, even with no income.

Read the full guide →

Pro forma Form 1120

A Form 1120 on which only basic details are completed, such as the LLC's name, address, and EIN. It is the cover to which Form 5472 is attached and does not mean the LLC owes corporate tax.

Read the full guide →

Information return

A return that reports facts to the IRS rather than calculating tax. Form 5472 and the FBAR are information returns, which is why they are due even when no tax is owed.

Reportable transaction

For a foreign-owned LLC, any amount paid or received between the company and a related party, including formation costs paid by the owner, money put in, money taken out, and loans. Each is reported on Form 5472.

Read the full guide →

Related party

For Form 5472, a person connected to the reporting company, such as a 25% foreign shareholder or a person related to it under the tax rules. For a single-member LLC, this is usually the owner.

25% foreign shareholder

A foreign person who owns, directly or indirectly, at least 25% of a corporation's voting power or value. For a foreign-owned LLC, the owner's details are reported in the 25% foreign shareholder part of Form 5472.

Capital contribution

Money or property the owner puts into the LLC, such as funding its bank account or paying its bills personally. For a foreign-owned LLC, contributions are reportable on Form 5472.

Distribution

Money or property the LLC pays out to its owner, such as transferring profits to the owner's personal account. For a foreign-owned LLC, distributions are reportable on Form 5472.

Form 1120

The U.S. corporation income tax return. A corporation files a full Form 1120, while a foreign-owned single-member LLC files only a pro forma version with Form 5472 attached.

Read the full guide →

Form 1065 and Schedule K-1

Form 1065 is the partnership return filed by most multi-member LLCs. Each owner receives a Schedule K-1 showing their share of income. Foreign partners can bring extra reporting and withholding.

Read the full guide →

Form 7004

The form that gives an automatic six-month extension to file business returns, including the pro forma Form 1120 with Form 5472. A foreign-owned LLC must fax or mail it by the original due date.

Read the full guide →

Form 5471

A different form, filed by U.S. persons who own or control foreign corporations. It is often confused with Form 5472, which covers foreign owners of U.S. companies.

Read the full guide →

Tax years and deadlines

Tax year

The 12-month period a return covers. Most LLCs use the calendar year, January to December. The Form 5472 return is due on the 15th day of the fourth month after the tax year ends: April 15 for calendar-year LLCs.

Read the full guide →

Fiscal year

A tax year that ends on the last day of any month other than December. A fiscal-year LLC's Form 5472 is due on the 15th day of the fourth month after its year ends.

Read the full guide →

Short tax year

A tax year shorter than 12 months, most often the first year, which runs from the formation date to December 31. It still needs a full Form 5472 filing.

Read the full guide →

Identification numbers and tax forms

EIN (Employer Identification Number)

The nine-digit federal tax number of your LLC. Every Form 5472 is filed under the LLC's EIN. Owners without an SSN or ITIN cannot apply online and usually apply by fax, mail, or phone.

Form SS-4

The application for an EIN. It names the LLC, its state, its owner, and its responsible party.

Responsible party

The individual who ultimately owns or controls the LLC, named on Form SS-4. A foreign owner can be the responsible party without a U.S. tax number.

ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number)

A U.S. tax number for individuals who cannot get an SSN. It is applied for on Form W-7, sometimes through a Certifying Acceptance Agent who verifies identity documents. You need one only if you must file a personal U.S. return or claim certain benefits, not for Form 5472.

Foreign taxpayer identification number (FTIN)

The tax number issued by your home country, such as a TIN, PAN, or KRA PIN. Form 5472 asks for the foreign owner's FTIN, and "None" or "N/A" is entered if there is none.

Form W-8BEN

The form a foreign individual gives to U.S. payers and platforms to confirm they are not a U.S. person and, where a treaty applies, to claim reduced withholding. The foreign owner of a disregarded LLC usually gives it in their own name.

Form W-9

The form U.S. persons give to payers to provide their U.S. tax number. A foreign owner should not submit it, as their income may then be reported as if they were a U.S. taxpayer.

U.S. income tax for nonresidents

Nonresident alien

An individual who is neither a U.S. citizen nor a U.S. tax resident. Nonresident aliens are taxed only on certain U.S. income, not on their worldwide income.

Substantial presence test

The day-count test that can make a foreign individual a U.S. tax resident: at least 31 days in the U.S. this year and 183 days over three years, counting all days this year, a third of last year's, and a sixth of the year before.

U.S. trade or business

Regular, ongoing business activity carried on in the United States. Whether your LLC has one decides whether its profits are taxed in the U.S.

Effectively connected income (ECI)

Income connected with a U.S. trade or business. A nonresident owner pays U.S. tax on it at regular rates and files Form 1040-NR. Services performed entirely outside the U.S. usually do not create ECI.

FDAP income and 30% withholding

Fixed, determinable, annual, or periodical income from U.S. sources, such as dividends, interest, and royalties. Paid to a foreign person, it faces 30% withholding unless a treaty or exemption reduces it.

Form 1042-S

The form a U.S. payer issues to report U.S.-source income paid to a foreign person and any tax withheld.

Form 1040-NR

The U.S. income tax return for nonresident individuals with U.S. taxable income. It is usually due April 15, or June 15 if you had no U.S. wages subject to withholding.

Tax treaty

An agreement between two countries that decides which country may tax certain income and can reduce U.S. withholding. A treaty never removes the Form 5472 filing.

Read the full guide →

Permanent establishment

A treaty term for a fixed place of business, such as an office, or an agent who regularly signs contracts for you. In treaty countries, business profits are generally taxed in the U.S. only if there is a permanent establishment there.

Read the full guide →

Bank and asset reporting

FBAR (FinCEN Form 114)

A report of foreign financial accounts, required from U.S. persons, including U.S. LLCs, whose accounts outside the U.S. total more than $10,000 at any time in the year. It is filed with FinCEN, not the IRS, and is due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.

Read the full guide →

FATCA and Form 8938

FATCA is a U.S. law requiring banks worldwide to report accounts held by U.S. persons. Form 8938 is the matching IRS form some U.S. taxpayers file for foreign assets above set thresholds. A foreign owner who is not a U.S. taxpayer generally does not file it.

BOI report (beneficial ownership information)

A report of a company's owners to FinCEN. In 2025, FinCEN exempted companies formed in the United States from this requirement. It is separate from Form 5472, which remains due.

Penalties and catch-up

Form 5472 penalty

$25,000 for each Form 5472 that is late, missing, or substantially incomplete, per year, plus $25,000 for each 30 days the failure continues more than 90 days after IRS notice. A return missing key information can be treated as not filed.

Read the full guide →

Reasonable cause

The main ground for penalty relief: showing that you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but still could not file on time. It is explained in a written statement signed under penalties of perjury.

Read the full guide →

Penalty abatement

The removal of a penalty by the IRS, most often for reasonable cause. First-time abatement applies to Form 5472 only in narrow cases. A refused request may be reviewed by the IRS Independent Office of Appeals.

Read the full guide →

Delinquent International Information Return Submission Procedures

An IRS route for filing late international information returns, such as Form 5472, with a reasonable cause statement, for taxpayers not already under examination. Penalties can still be assessed.

Read the full guide →

Statute of limitations

The period in which the IRS can assess tax, usually three years. If a required information return such as Form 5472 is not filed, the period for that year generally does not start until the information is provided.

Read the full guide →

State terms

Annual report

A yearly filing with your LLC's state that confirms its details and keeps it active. Wyoming's is due by the first day of the month the LLC was formed, and Florida's between January 1 and May 1. New Mexico does not require one from LLCs.

Read the full guide →

Franchise tax

A yearly state tax for the right to exist in the state, often owed even without income. Delaware charges LLCs an annual tax due June 1.

Read the full guide →

Good standing

The status of an LLC that is current with its state filings and fees. Banks and payment platforms may ask for a certificate of good standing.

Read the full guide →

Administrative dissolution

When a state closes an LLC for missing its annual report or fees. A dissolved LLC can lose its name and good standing, and reinstatement usually costs extra.

Read the full guide →

Sales tax nexus

A connection with a U.S. state, such as sales above a state's threshold or inventory stored there, that can require an online seller to register for and collect that state's sales tax. It is separate from federal income tax.
