U.S. LLC Taxes for Nigerian Founders: What to File Each Year

By Meritline TaxUpdated October 20, 20269 min read

Key points

  • A U.S. LLC owned by one person living in Nigeria generally files Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120 every year, even when it earns nothing.
  • The return is due April 15 for calendar-year LLCs, or October 15 with a Form 7004 extension. It is sent to the IRS by fax or mail and can be done entirely from Nigeria.
  • The penalty for a late or incomplete Form 5472 is $25,000 per form, per year.
  • There is no income tax treaty between Nigeria and the United States, which affects withholding, the forms you give U.S. platforms, and how the U.S. decides whether you owe tax.
  • Nigeria's tax system changed on January 1, 2026, under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025, and the federal tax authority is now the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS).

Nigerian freelancers, software developers, agencies, and e-commerce sellers are among the most active users of U.S. LLCs. A Wyoming, Delaware, or New Mexico company makes it easier to invoice international clients in dollars, work with U.S. payment processors and marketplaces, and present a business that overseas customers recognize. Forming the company takes days. The yearly compliance that follows is rarely explained, and it carries one of the largest penalties in the U.S. tax code. This guide covers what a Nigerian owner needs to file each year, how the absence of a tax treaty affects you, and the practical points that come up when you run a U.S. company from Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, or anywhere else in Nigeria.

The yearly federal filing: Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120

When a U.S. LLC has a single owner who is not a U.S. person, the IRS treats it as a foreign-owned disregarded entity. For income tax it is ignored, but for reporting purposes it is treated as a corporation. Each year it must file Form 5472, attached to a pro forma Form 1120, reporting the transactions between the company and you as its owner.

It is an information return: it does not calculate tax. The return is filed under the LLC's EIN, cannot be e-filed, and is faxed or mailed to the IRS. For a full explanation, read Form 5472 Explained, and for the dates that apply to your company, see Form 5472 tax years and due dates.

How Nigerian owners typically create reportable transactions

Almost any movement of money or value between you and the LLC must be reported. The way money moves for Nigerian founders creates some recurring patterns:

  • Formation and agent fees paid personally. Because naira cards often carry low or restricted limits for international payments, many owners pay the formation company, registered agent, or state fees from a personal domiciliary account, a virtual dollar card, or a fintech wallet. Each payment is a contribution to the company.
  • Funding the LLC's account. Sending dollars from your personal account to the LLC's U.S. bank or payment account, for example to cover software subscriptions or advertising, is a reportable contribution.
  • Moving profit home. Transferring money from the LLC to your personal domiciliary or naira account is a distribution to you.
  • Mixed personal and business spending. Using the LLC's card for personal costs, or your personal card for company costs, creates transactions that must be recorded.

Keep a simple record as you go. Note the date, amount in dollars, and purpose of every transfer between you and the company, including transfers routed through fintech apps or currency exchange services. Reconstructing a year of mixed transactions later is far harder than keeping a running list.

What having no tax treaty means for you

The United States and Nigeria do not have an income tax treaty in force. In practice, this has three consequences:

  • The U.S. applies its ordinary rules to decide whether you owe tax. Treaty countries benefit from a "permanent establishment" threshold before U.S. tax applies. Without a treaty, the test is whether you are engaged in a U.S. trade or business, which can be met more easily. Many Nigerian owners run their business entirely from Nigeria, with no U.S. office, employees, dependent agents, or inventory, and owe no U.S. income tax. Using U.S.-based staff or agents, or holding stock in a U.S. warehouse, can change that.
  • No reduced withholding rates. Certain U.S.-source income, such as dividends, interest that does not qualify for an exemption, and royalties, is subject to the standard 30% withholding rate, with no treaty reduction available.
  • No treaty claim on your tax forms. When you complete a Form W-8BEN, the treaty section is left blank, because there is no Nigeria treaty to claim.

Whether or not you owe U.S. income tax, the Form 5472 filing still applies.

The tax forms U.S. platforms will ask you for

Payment processors, marketplaces, and U.S. clients usually ask for a tax form before they pay you. Because your LLC is disregarded and you are its foreign owner, the correct form is generally a Form W-8BEN in your own name, as a non-U.S. individual, not a Form W-9. Supplying a W-9 can result in your income being reported to the IRS as if you were a U.S. taxpayer, while an incorrect W-8 can cause unnecessary withholding.

Filing from Nigeria in practice

  • Getting an EIN without an SSN. Non-U.S. owners cannot use the IRS online EIN application. The usual route is Form SS-4, sent to the IRS by fax or mail, or an application made by telephone through the IRS line for international applicants.
  • No visa, SSN, or ITIN needed for Form 5472. The return is filed under the LLC's EIN. An ITIN is only needed if you personally have to file a U.S. income tax return.
  • Sending the return. The pro forma Form 1120 and Form 5472 are faxed to the IRS at the number given in the current instructions, or mailed to the IRS in Ogden, Utah. Most Nigerian owners use an online fax service rather than a physical fax machine. Keep the transmission confirmation.
  • Mind the time difference. U.S. deadlines run on U.S. time, which is five to six hours behind Nigeria. Aim to file several days early rather than late in the evening, Nigerian time, on the due date.
  • Your Nigerian tax number. Form 5472 asks for the owner's foreign taxpayer identification number. If you have a Nigerian tax identification number, have it ready.

State filings for Nigerian-owned LLCs

Your LLC also answers to the state where it was formed. The three states most often chosen by Nigerian founders have different rules:

StateYearly state requirement
WyomingAnnual report and license tax, due on the first day of the month the LLC was formed.
DelawareAnnual LLC tax, due June 1.
New MexicoNo annual report for LLCs. Keep your registered agent current.

Missing a state filing can lead to late fees and, in time, administrative dissolution of the company. See our state filings guide.

Other filings to know about

  • FBAR. A U.S. LLC is a U.S. person for this purpose. If it holds financial accounts outside the United States, for example an account in Nigeria in the company's name, with more than $10,000 combined at any point in the year, an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is due.
  • BOI report. At the time of writing, companies formed in the United States are exempt from FinCEN's beneficial ownership reporting. This does not affect Form 5472, which is a separate IRS requirement.

The Nigerian side

Nigeria taxes its residents on their income, which can include profits earned through a foreign company. Since January 1, 2026, Nigeria's main tax laws have been consolidated under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025, and the Federal Inland Revenue Service has been replaced by the Nigeria Revenue Service. Personal income tax for individuals continues to be administered by the tax authority of your state of residence.

The reforms also broaden when a company is treated as Nigerian, including by reference to where it is effectively managed and controlled. If you run your U.S. LLC from Nigeria, ask a Nigerian tax adviser how its profits should be reported at home, and whether the new rules affect your structure. If you have missed earlier years

Many Nigerian owners discover Form 5472 a year or two after forming their company, often from a formation agent's reminder email or an online forum. Each missed year is a separate filing. The strongest position is to file every outstanding year before the IRS makes contact, with a reasonable cause statement where the facts support one. Read what to do if you missed a Form 5472 deadline, or see our late filings and catch-up service.

What to keep ready each year

  • The LLC's Articles of Organization and EIN confirmation letter
  • Your passport details, Nigerian residential address, and Nigerian tax identification number if you have one
  • Statements for every LLC bank, payment, and marketplace account
  • A dated list of money you put into the company and money you took out
  • Receipts for company costs paid from your personal domiciliary account, dollar card, or fintech wallet

Common questions from Nigerian owners

Do I need a U.S. visa, SSN, or ITIN to file from Nigeria?

No. Form 5472 and the pro forma Form 1120 are filed under the LLC's EIN, which can be obtained without a Social Security Number. An ITIN is only needed if you personally must file a U.S. income tax return.

Is there a tax treaty between Nigeria and the United States?

No. There is no income tax treaty in force between the two countries, so the ordinary U.S. rules for non-resident owners apply, including the standard 30% withholding rate on certain U.S.-source payments and no treaty claim on Form W-8BEN.

My LLC made no money this year. Do I still file?

Most likely, yes. Form 5472 reports transactions between you and the company, not profit. Paying the formation fee or funding the account yourself is usually enough to create a filing requirement.

I paid company costs from my domiciliary account. Does that matter?

Yes. Paying company costs from a personal account, dollar card, or fintech wallet is a contribution from you to the LLC, and it should be reported on Form 5472 for the year it happened.

Can the return be filed from Nigeria without travelling?

Yes. The return is faxed or mailed to the IRS, and you do not need to be in the United States. You share your documents online, and we confirm once the return has been sent.
